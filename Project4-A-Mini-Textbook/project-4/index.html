The Skateboard Mystery: Make a Prediction

Imagine you are standing on a skateboard on completely flat, smooth pavement. Next to you is your friend, also standing on a skateboard. Neither of you is touching the ground with your feet.

You reach out your hands and give your friend a firm push forward.

Predict

Stop & Predict: What will happen to you? What will happen to your friend?

  • A) Only your friend rolls forward; you stay completely still because you did the pushing.
  • B) Your friend rolls forward, and you roll backward.
  • C) Both of you roll forward in the direction you pushed.
2

Testing the Prediction: What Actually Happens?

Test

If you try this experiment on skateboards, roller skates, or ice skates, you will observe something unmistakable: Both skaters move.

Even though you exerted the effort and pushed forward on your friend, you are propelled backward simultaneously.

Explain

Why? You cannot push on something without it pushing back on you. In physics, a force isn't an object that someone possesses; a force is an interaction between two objects.

When Object A applies a force to Object B, Object B automatically and simultaneously applies an equal force back on Object A in the opposite direction.

The Skater Interaction Pair

Notice: Each arrow starts on one skater and points to the other. The two forces act on two different people.

3

Formalizing the Law: Newton's Third Law

Sir Isaac Newton formulated this discovery as his Third Law of Motion:

FA on B = −FB on A
FA on B
"The force that Object A exerts on Object B."
FB on A
"The force that Object B exerts on Object A."
Minus sign (−)
The two forces point in exact opposite directions.
Equals sign (=)
Both forces have the exact same strength (magnitude).
Important

Action and Reaction are Simultaneous! While everyday language says "For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction," do not think of this like a cause and a delayed effect. "Action" and "reaction" happen at the exact same instant as parts of a single mutual interaction. Neither force exists before or after the other.

4

The Big Misconception: Why Don't They Cancel Out?

A very common question is:

Misconception
"If every force has an equal and opposite force, why does anything ever move? Shouldn't the forces add up to zero and cancel each other out?"
Why it's wrong: Forces only cancel out when they act on the same object.

Example of cancellation (Newton's 1st/2nd Law): Two people push a heavy box from opposite sides with 50 N each. Both forces are applied to the box. ΣF = 50 N − 50 N = 0 N. The box does not accelerate.

Correct idea: Newton's Third Law Pair: Force 1 is Skater 1 pushing on Skater 2. Force 2 is Skater 2 pushing on Skater 1. Because Force 1 is felt by Skater 2, and Force 2 is felt by Skater 1, each individual skater experiences an unbalanced net force and accelerates!
5

The Bug vs. The Windshield: Equal Forces, Unequal Effects

Predict

A highway truck traveling at 65 mph collides with a tiny mosquito hovering in the air. Which experiences a greater force during the collision: the truck or the mosquito?

Explain

According to Newton's Third Law, the force exerted by the truck on the mosquito is EXACTLY EQUAL to the force exerted by the mosquito on the truck.

Collision Force Pair

Why does the mosquito suffer catastrophic damage while the truck barely registers a bump? Recall Newton's Second Law (a = F/m):

Mass & Acceleration

Same Force, Different Results

For the mosquito (tiny mass m)
amosquito = F / tiny m → Enormous acceleration (fatal)
For the truck (huge mass M)
atruck = F / huge M → Negligible acceleration

Chapter Recap

Forces are Interactions

A force never exists alone; it is always a mutual push or pull between two distinct objects.

Newton's Third Law

Whenever Object A exerts a force on Object B, Object B simultaneously exerts an equal-sized force on Object A in the opposite direction (FA on B = −FB on A).

Why They Don't Cancel

Action-reaction pairs act on different objects, meaning each individual object feels a net force that can cause it to accelerate.

Forces vs. Accelerations

Equal forces do not mean equal results. By Newton's Second Law (a = F/m), an object with tiny mass experiences a huge acceleration, while an object with huge mass experiences very little acceleration from the same force.
